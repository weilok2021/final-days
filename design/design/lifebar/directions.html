Lifebar: four ways to keep the number in view

The book's point is that a life is about 30,000 days and today is one of them. The software's only job is to make sure you never forget it. Below are the candidate forms, each rendered on a mock 1920×1080 desktop. Put in your date of birth; every mockup updates. Lifespan is fixed at 80 years and is not a user setting.

A. Edge strip recommended, always on

A 4-pixel bar along the very top of the primary monitor, filled from the left as life passes. The colour runs green at birth, through yellow at mid-life, to red at the end; the part not yet lived is grey. So at 30 the visible fill is green, at 45 it has reached yellow, at 65 it is turning red. No icon, no text. Hovering the strip shows one line of numbers.

Why "reserved edge" matters: the top-left corner of the screen at 4× zoom

A maximized window (the square button, app fills the screen) normally covers everything except the taskbar. Windows keeps the taskbar's area reserved so no app can cover it. The strip asks Windows for the same treatment at the top edge. Result: the screen behaves as if it were 1920×1076, the top 4 pixels belong to the bar, and nothing ever overlaps in either direction.

B. Daily moment recommended, once a day

A persistent bar fades into the wallpaper within a week; everyone stops seeing what never changes. The fix is one deliberate moment per day: the first time you unlock the PC each morning, the whole screen goes dark and shows today's number. One click dismisses it. It costs about three seconds and it is the thing that actually re-installs the fact in your head.

C. Wallpaper grid alternative or bonus

Your life as a grid of weeks, one square per week, 52 per row, regenerated as the desktop wallpaper every night. Very striking, and free to add once the numbers exist. Its weakness is that a maximized window hides it completely, so it cannot be the primary carrier on its own.

one square = one week · squares left

D. Friction gate optional app-block layer

Instead of hard-blocking apps (which people switch off within a week), the lifebar steps in front of a listed app when it comes to the foreground. It shows the cost and makes you wait ten seconds before the "open anyway" button works. Close it, or go through consciously. The list of apps and sites is yours to set; a hard-block mode can be added later if the pause is not enough.

Recommendation

  1. Build A + B together as one tiny always-running program. A keeps the fact in the corner of your eye all day; B makes sure you actually read it once a day. Neither works well alone.
  2. Add C for free once the numbers exist: a nightly wallpaper render is twenty lines.
  3. Do D as a second phase, only after living with A + B for two weeks and knowing which apps actually eat the days.

Where and when, in one place

WhereTop edge of the primary monitor, full width, 4 px tall, as a reserved edge. Not the taskbar (already crowded), not the tray (icons get hidden), not the wallpaper alone (covered).
WhenStrip: always, from login. Moment: first unlock of each calendar day. Gate: whenever a listed app comes to the foreground.
What it showsStrip: green-to-red fill for life passed, grey for life left. Hover: day N of total, days left. Moment: days left in large type, then "Is today worth one of your remaining N days?"
InputsDate of birth only, set once in a small config file. Lifespan is a constant of 80 years in the code, not a setting.
